# Exercise design

## Parametric curves {#parametric-curves}

### Connection to the lecture

The lecture moves from a line with constant direction to a curve generated by $x=x(t)$ and $y=y(t)$.

### Boundary of the lecture

Only one circle parametrization and a few special parameter values are calculated.

### Work transferred to exercises

Students should generate point tables, draw simple parametric curves, compare two parametrizations of the same set, examine the effect of parameter range and verify points in a Cartesian equation.

### Planned problem types

- point generation from elementary parametrizations;
- one same-curve/different-parametrization comparison;
- one restricted-parameter problem;
- one Cartesian verification problem;
- one simple visualization.

### Required workbook result

Tables, graphs, substitution checks and a statement distinguishing the geometric curve from the way its points are ordered or traversed.

### Verification

Every claimed parametrization must be checked against a Cartesian condition or a second representation.

## Parameters and shape {#parameters}

### Connection to the lecture

The lecture uses sliders to distinguish parameters selecting a point from parameters changing the position, scale or shape of an entire curve.

### Boundary of the lecture

The lecture reads only a few transparent examples. Systematic comparison of classical forms is transferred.

### Work transferred to exercises

Students should:

1. recognize elementary Cartesian forms of the circle, ellipse, parabola and hyperbola;
2. read centres, shifts and scales from simple equations;
3. vary one parameter while fixing the others;
4. build a “parameter — geometric effect” table;
5. compare equations differing only by translation or scaling;
6. verify selected points by substitution.

### Planned problem types

- identification and reading tasks;
- a sequence of controlled graphs;
- one comparison problem;
- one explanation of the different roles of $t$ and a shape parameter.

### Required workbook result

A family of graphs, a parameter-effect table, numerical checks and a short interpretation.

### Verification

Predicted changes must be compared with generated graphs and at least one point on each curve must be checked algebraically.

## Radial conic family {#radial-family}

### Connection to the lecture

The lecture introduces radial coordinates and the family

$$
r(\theta)=\frac{p}{1+e\cos\theta},
$$

using animation to show how eccentricity changes the type of conic.

### Boundary of the lecture

Only a few values of $r(\theta)$ are computed by hand. Detailed comparison and student-built visualizations move to exercises.

### Work transferred to exercises

Students should calculate selected radii, compare $e=0$, $0<e<1$, $e=1$ and $e>1$, describe the approach to the boundary value $e=1$, compare radial and Cartesian descriptions and explain why a radial language is natural for a distinguished centre.

### Planned problem types

- small-value calculations at special angles;
- a four-case comparison table;
- graph generation;
- one representation-comparison problem;
- one student-prepared interactive visualization or carefully labelled sequence of figures.

### Required workbook result

Calculations, classification table, graphs and an interpretation of eccentricity. AI-generated code must be checked on explicit parameter values and corrected by the student when necessary.

### Verification

The visualized curve must agree with the computed radii at selected angles.

## Focus and directrix {#focus-directrix}

### Connection to the lecture

The focus-directrix condition is introduced after the radial family to explain the geometric meaning of eccentricity.

### Boundary of the lecture

The lecture animates one point and one transparent parabola case. It does not derive every Cartesian equation.

### Work transferred to exercises

Students should calculate distances to a focus and a directrix, test the ratio for several points, compare $e<1$, $e=1$ and $e>1$, reconstruct a qualitative curve from geometric data and distinguish coordinate-dependent descriptions from geometric elements.

### Planned problem types

- direct distance checks;
- a ratio table;
- one geometric construction;
- one comparison across three eccentricity regimes;
- one short argument about invariance.

### Required workbook result

A labelled focus-directrix diagram, distance calculations, constant-ratio checks and a geometric conclusion.

### Verification

The ratio must be evaluated at more than one point; one isolated numerical coincidence is insufficient.

## Mathematical model {#model}

### Connection to the lecture

The lecture distinguishes three levels: the shape of a trajectory, a parametrized motion along it and the physical law selecting that motion.

### Boundary of the lecture

No derivation of orbital mechanics is attempted. The lecture only shows the architecture of the model and a qualitative orbit classification.

### Work transferred to exercises

Students should:

1. classify simple trajectories by eccentricity;
2. separate geometric statements from physical assumptions;
3. compare two motions along the same geometric curve;
4. identify what information a static orbit equation does not provide;
5. analyse a deliberately oversimplified model and list its assumptions;
6. prepare a short presentation showing the hierarchy “curve — parametrization — physical law”.

### Planned problem types

- qualitative classification;
- one model-assumption audit;
- one same-path/different-motion comparison;
- one diagrammatic presentation;
- optionally, one simple data-to-parameter inference problem.

### Required workbook result

A three-level model diagram, a table of assumptions, a qualitative classification and a short statement explaining why a conic equation alone is not a complete theory of gravity.

### Verification

Students must give an example of two different parametrizations producing the same trajectory, or otherwise demonstrate that shape does not determine motion.

## Set audit {#set-audit}

The set must not become a catalogue of four unrelated conics. Every task should reinforce at least one of three ideas: a curve is generated, a parameter organizes a family, or a mathematical model separates geometry from dynamics and physical assumptions.
